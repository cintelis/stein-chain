Why post-quantum, and why now

Most blockchains are secured by elliptic-curve signatures. A large enough quantum computer breaks those, and a public ledger cannot take its history back.

The signatures break

Shor's algorithm recovers a private key from an elliptic-curve public key. That covers ECDSA, EdDSA and BLS: the schemes behind nearly every chain in use today.

The ledger is permanent

Public keys published on a chain stay there. An account that has ever spent has its key on record, waiting for the day the mathematics stops protecting it.

Migration is the hard part

Moving a live chain to new signatures means every holder acting before the deadline. Stein starts where that migration ends: there is no classical scheme to retire.

How Stein is built

Post-quantum signatures are far larger than the ones they replace. The design is about keeping transactions small and trusting nothing that has not been checked.

Signatures

Two post-quantum families

FN-DSA-512 (draft FIPS 206) is the default for accounts: 666-byte signatures. ML-DSA (FIPS 204) is accepted for accounts and required for validators.

Verification

Every node checks everything

A node verifies every signature in a block, and the resulting state, before it accepts the block. Nothing a block producer claims is taken on trust.

Size

790-byte transfers

An account's public key goes on chain once, with its first transaction. Every later transfer carries a signature only, and fees are charged per byte.

Agility

Ready for the next standard

Every key and signature carries a scheme tag. A new standard is added as a new tag, with no change of format, and accounts on different schemes transact freely.

Hashing

BLAKE3, separated by purpose

Every hash and every signed message has its own domain, so a value made for one purpose can never be replayed as another. Transaction ids cover the body, not the signature.

Transport

Post-quantum between nodes

Nodes talk over TLS 1.3 with a hybrid ML-KEM key exchange, so traffic recorded today cannot be decrypted by a quantum computer later.

Where it stands

Stein is in development. This is what exists today and what comes next.

Built
  • Devnet with post-quantum transactions, accounts and fees
  • FN-DSA-512 and ML-DSA accounts on one chain
  • Follower nodes that re-verify every block, over post-quantum TLS
  • steinscan, the live block explorer
  • steinwallet, a browser wallet that signs in WebAssembly
  • A command-line wallet and node, in Rust
Next
  • Hardening: fuzzing, state on disk, RPC rate limits
  • Peer-to-peer networking and transaction gossip
  • Proof-of-stake consensus with deterministic finality, formally modelled
  • Public testnet, faucet and wallet SDK
  • Smart contracts in WebAssembly
  • Independent audits, then mainnet

Today the devnet has a single validator, and its STEIN has no value. It can be reset at any time. Nothing here is an offer of a token or an investment.
